0 · Evolution strategies in plain words

Imagine you are on a hillside in thick fog and want to reach the bottom of the valley. You can't see the slope. What you can do is send a few friends a couple of steps in random directions and ask each one, "how high are you now?"

  1. Ask. Send friends out in random directions from where you stand. These are the candidates.
  2. Evaluate. Each friend reports their altitude. In machine learning this is the loss: how wrong a network is.
  3. Shape. Turn the reports into votes. Often only the ranking matters ("Ana was lowest, Ben second…"), so one wild report can't fool you.
  4. Tell. Take a step toward the friends who were lowest. Repeat.

That is an evolution strategy. Here the "position" is the network's 17 knobs (weights), a "friend" is a copy of the network with slightly nudged knobs, and "altitude" is the loss on the coloured dots.

Why not just compute the slope? When you can, you should. That is backpropagation, and it is very efficient. But sometimes the slope doesn't exist or is hard to compute: a robot in a physics simulator, a game score, a rounded or discrete decision. Evolution only needs to run the thing and read a score. Every friend can also be tested at the same time on a different computer.

1 · The network and the task

Every algorithm on this page trains the same model: a two-layer perceptron with 2 inputs, 4 tanh hidden neurons and 1 sigmoid output. It classifies points in the plane.

z₁ = W₁ x + b₁        (4×2 matrix, 4 biases)
h  = tanh(z₁)
z₂ = W₂ h + b₂        (1×4 matrix, 1 bias)
p  = σ(z₂)            probability of class 1
L  = −mean[ y log p + (1−y) log(1−p) ]

That is 17 numbers in total. Each hidden neuron draws one straight line across the plane. tanh turns that line into a soft step: +1 on one side, −1 on the other. The output adds up four such steps. Four lines are enough to carve XOR or a rough circle. Training means finding 17 numbers that put the lines in the right places.

The thumbnails inside the network diagram are the key to reading it. Like the activation maps in CNN Explainer, each one shows what that neuron outputs for every possible input, not just the selected point.

2 · One loop for every algorithm

evosax writes every evolution strategy as the same ask–evaluate–tell loop:

es = CMA_ES(population_size=32, solution=dummy_solution)
params = es.default_params
state = es.init(key, dummy_solution, params)

for i in range(num_generations):
    key, key_ask, key_eval = jax.random.split(key, 3)
    population, state = es.ask(key_ask, state, params)     # 1. propose candidates
    fitness = ...                                           # 2. forward passes
    state, metrics = es.tell(key, population, fitness, state, params)  # 3+4. shape & update

The playground splits tell into two boxes. Shape turns raw fitness into weights (evosax passes a fitness_shaping_fn to each algorithm). Tell uses those weights to update the state. Here fitness is the loss, so lower is better.

Backpropagation fits the same mold. Its "population" is a single network, "shape" is the backward pass, and "tell" is a gradient step. Seeing them side by side makes the difference clear: backprop asks the network which direction is downhill, and evolution strategies find out by trying directions and comparing.

4 · The families

Exact gradient · Backprop + gradient descent

One forward pass stores the activations. The backward pass applies the chain rule from the loss back to every weight: δ₂ = p − y, δ₁ = δ₂·W₂ ⊙ (1 − h²), ∂L/∂W = δ·aᵀ. Then θ ← θ − η∇L.

This is exact and cheap: one forward and one backward pass, whatever the number of parameters. But it needs a differentiable model and loss, and it only sees the local slope. Turn on show backprop gradients to see δ on each neuron and ∂L/∂w on each edge.

ES · gradient estimators (OpenES, PGPE, EGGROLL)

These methods blur the loss with Gaussian noise and descend the blurred version. Its gradient can be estimated without differentiating anything: ∇E[L(μ+σε)] = E[L(μ+σε)·ε]/σ. Sample ε, run forward passes, and weight each ε by how good it was (Salimans et al., 2017).

Mirrored sampling evaluates +ε and −ε, which cancels much of the noise. Centered ranks replace losses by ranks, so outliers can't dominate. The estimate is passed to an ordinary optimizer such as Adam. After each Tell, the playground prints the cosine similarity between this estimate and the true backprop gradient.

PGPE (Sehnke et al., 2010) also learns a separate σ for every parameter. If a symmetric pair both did better than average, wider search along that direction pays off.

ES · EGGROLL (low-rank perturbations)

From "Evolution Strategies at the Hyperscale" (Sarkar et al., 2025). For a big layer W ∈ ℝm×n, every population member needs its own m×n noise matrix, and every forward pass is a separate matrix multiply. EGGROLL replaces the noise with a low-rank product E = A Bᵀ/√r, where A ∈ ℝm×r and B ∈ ℝn×r.

(W + σ A Bᵀ) x  =  W x  +  σ A (Bᵀ x)
   shared by the whole population ↑     ↑ only r extra numbers per member

Each perturbation is rank r, but the update sums many of them, so its rank can reach min(N·r, m, n). The paper shows the update approaches full-rank OpenES as r grows. Here r = 1 on tiny 4×2 and 1×4 layers, so nothing is saved, but the structure is visible: select an EGGROLL member to see its W₁ noise as a·bᵀ. EGGROLL is not part of evosax; this is a minimal re-implementation of the idea.

ES · natural gradient (SNES)

Natural Evolution Strategies (Wierstra et al., 2014) treat the search distribution's parameters as what is being optimized. They follow the natural gradient, which measures steps by how much the distribution changes rather than by raw parameter distance. For a separable Gaussian (SNES, Schaul et al., 2011) this gives two simple rules: μ moves by σ ⊙ Σ uᵢzᵢ, and σ is multiplied by exp(η/2 · Σ uᵢ(zᵢ² − 1)). The uᵢ are rank-based utilities. xNES is the full-covariance version.

ES · recombination & adaptation (SimpleES, CMA-ES)

These methods estimate no gradient. They recombine: the new center is a weighted average of the best half. SimpleES then sets each σ from the elite's spread.

CMA-ES (Hansen, tutorial) learns a full covariance matrix C, so the sampling cloud can stretch along directions that keep paying off. Two evolution paths remember recent steps. One drives a rank-one update of C; the other grows σ when consecutive steps point the same way and shrinks it when they cancel. The correlation heat-map under the parameters shows what C has learned. Storing C costs O(D²) memory, which is why Sep-CMA-ES and other diagonal variants exist for large networks.

Population-based (SimpleGA, DE, PSO)

These methods keep actual individuals instead of a distribution.

Genetic algorithms select the best (truncation), then breed them with crossover and mutation. Such et al. (2017) showed this simple scheme can train deep RL policies.

Differential Evolution (Storn & Price, 1997) mutates with the difference of two other members. Step sizes therefore shrink by themselves as the population converges. A trial replaces its parent only if it is at least as good.

Particle Swarm (Kennedy & Eberhart, 1995) gives each particle a velocity pulled toward its own best spot and the swarm's best spot.

Zeroth-order · Dust (node perturbation)

"Dust: Pretraining Transformers Without Backpropagation" (Dahal, Mandal, Gülbahar & Vegesna, Q Labs, 2026). Every method above jitters the weights, so each candidate is a whole copy of the network and costs a whole forward pass. Dust jitters the neurons instead. This old idea, called node perturbation, is scaled up here to language-model pretraining.

  1. Jitter the neurons, not the weights. Run the normal forward pass, but add a small random number σ·a to each neuron's output. Use fresh numbers for every data point (in the paper: every token) and for each of K draws.
  2. Count virtual candidates. Each (draw, data point) pair acts as one population member. Here 16 draws × 80 dots give 1,280 members. In a transformer with 16,384 tokens per batch, this makes populations of millions affordable.
  3. Score each jitter locally. For one data point, compare a draw's loss with the average loss of all draws on that same point: c = ℓ̄ − ℓ. A positive c means this jitter helped.
  4. Turn helpful jitter into an error signal. Average the jitters weighted by how much they helped: ĝ = −1/(Kσ) Σ c·a. This estimates what backprop's backward pass would compute at that neuron, "you should have been a bit higher or lower", without running anything backwards.
  5. Finish like backprop. A weight's gradient is the neuron's error signal times the weight's input, GW = Σ ĝ·xᵀ, the same local rule backprop ends with. Then an ordinary optimizer step (SGD with momentum).

Compared with the boxes above, Dust keeps one weight vector, like backprop (no distribution, no population of networks). Its ask step puts the noise on activations, and its shape step scores each data point separately. In the playground, select a Dust draw: each neuron then shows the jitter it received for the selected dot.

In the paper, a 37.7M-parameter transformer trained this way reaches a test loss of 4.80 at 20M tokens, against 4.63 for backprop. The authors report it as orders of magnitude more efficient than weight-space ES such as EGGROLL. Attention layers also pass credit along the sequence with a decay γ; our tiny network has no sequence, so γ = 0. The 2-4-1 adaptation here is our own simplification.

Beyond fixed weights · NEAT

NeuroEvolution of Augmenting Topologies (Stanley & Miikkulainen, 2002) evolves the architecture as well as the weights. Every algorithm above searches over a fixed 17-number vector. NEAT's genome is a list of connection genes that grows over time.

  • Start minimal, complexify. Inputs connect straight to outputs. Hidden nodes appear only through mutation, so NEAT tends to find small networks.
  • Add-node splits a connection. The old gene is disabled, the incoming weight is 1 and the outgoing weight copies the old weight, so the network's behaviour barely changes at first.
  • Innovation numbers make crossover between different topologies possible. Genes with the same number are aligned; the rest are disjoint or excess.
  • Speciation groups genomes by δ = c₁E/N + c₂D/N + c₃·W̄ (excess, disjoint, mean weight difference). Fitness is shared within a species, so a new structure gets time to tune its weights before it has to compete.

Compared with the boxes above: the state is an explicit population (like a GA), and ask is crossover plus structural mutation. Shape is fitness sharing within species, and tell is selection per species. The forward pass runs over whatever graph the genome encodes, not a fixed 2-4-1 net. On XOR, NEAT typically ends up with only one or two hidden nodes.

5 · Backprop vs. evolution strategies

Backprop + GDEvolution strategies
NeedsA differentiable model and lossOnly forward passes and a score. The model can be non-differentiable, discrete, or a whole simulator.
SignalExact local gradient of this lossGradient of a smoothed loss, or just a ranking of candidates
Cost per update1 forward + 1 backward passN forward passes, but no backward pass and no stored activations
ParallelismWorkers exchange gradients of size DWorkers exchange scalar fitness values; noise can be regenerated from shared random seeds
ExplorationFollows the slope into the nearest basinσ smooths away small bumps; populations can explore several basins
Scaling with DExcellentGradient-estimate variance grows with D. Hence tricks like low-rank noise (EGGROLL), diagonal covariances and huge populations.
